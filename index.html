Japan Energy Futures Simulator

Projects Japan's electricity generation mix and grid carbon intensity through 2040, built on real 2000–2024 history (EIA, Ember) and literature-based emission factors. Move the controls below to choose how much nuclear power returns, how fast renewables grow, and how fast coal is phased down — the chart and figures update to match.

Methodology notes

How this model works, and what it assumes

Projection. Starting from 2024's actual generation mix, nuclear generation moves linearly toward your chosen target (expressed as a % of 2010's pre-Fukushima peak of 278 TWh). Coal declines and renewables grow at your chosen compound annual rate. Oil, other fossil gases, and hydroelectric are held at 2024 levels — all three are small and structurally fairly fixed in Japan's system. Natural gas absorbs whatever is left to meet total demand, held flat at the 2024 level — the same role it actually played after Fukushima. If a scenario would push non-gas generation above total demand, renewable growth is capped so gas is never implied to go negative; this only triggers under fairly extreme slider combinations.

Emission factors, in gCO2eq per kWh:

Nuclear12.5Hydroelectric24.9
Non-hydro renewables98.7Natural gas467.6
Other fossil gases675.4Oil727.4
Coal935.2

These are literature-standard (IPCC-style median) lifecycle factors, with the renewables figure blended to reflect Japan's actual solar/biomass/wind/geothermal split. A single uniform +3.9% scaling correction was fitted to account for accounting-scope differences between source datasets.

Validation. Checked against 25 years of actual history, this model achieves R² = 0.978 and a mean error of about 1.4%. It's a simplified scenario-exploration tool, not a precision forecast — treat projected numbers as directionally meaningful, not an exact prediction.

Import dependency. Coal, natural gas, oil, and other fossil gases are classified as imported, since Japan imports nearly all of its fossil fuel. Hydroelectric and renewables are classified as domestic. Nuclear is classified as quasi-domestic (準国産エネルギー), matching Japan's own METI convention — enriched uranium fuel can be stockpiled for years and sourced from diversified suppliers, so it's treated differently from continuously-flowing fossil fuel imports despite not being mined domestically.
